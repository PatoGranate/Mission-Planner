If two random satellites are put into orbit around the earth, the probability that they will be able to see each other at any given time is not much. To begin with, the earth is a large obstacle, which may easily block the satellites line of sight (LOS) of each other, and even when this doesn't occur, the satellites may well be many kms away from each other, so while they may be within direct LOS, they will not actually be able to see one another. 

Straightforwardly, we can check for these two conditions being met (given a specific threshold of visibility), and when they are being met at the same time, we can have satellite targeting.

### Distance checks
Checking for distance is very easy. Taking the coordinates of both satellites and taking the difference between the two will give the resulting connecting vector. The magnitude of this vector gives the distance straight up, which can be compared to the tolerance up to which the optics can capture images with sufficient resolution.

### Position checks
Checking for the position of the earth is not as straightforward but can still be done relatively simply. Consider two coordinates representing the spatial positions of either satellite. The shortest distance between the connecting vector and the centre of the earth is drawn by a line which is perpendicular to the vector and crosses the coordinate (0, 0, 0). 

A basic requirement of any satellite is that it's altitude is greater than 0 (i.e. its distance from the centre of the earth is greater than the radius of the earth). With this in mind it is evident that if there is no perpendicular line which crosses the connecting vector within the limits of either satellite (i.e. between the two satellites, not extrapolated in any direction), there is a direct line of sight between the two. 

 If, however, the perpendicular line lies between the two points, a second check must be performed to ensure that the magnitude of that line is larger than the radius of the earth, such that the earth does not cross the connecting vector. 

### Joining these conditions in Python
If both of these conditions are met, then we can safely say that there is a direct LOS between the satellites and that they are close enough to resolve each other.

So the distance of a point along that line can be computed as $rA + td$. Where t is a scalar and d is the magnitude of $VecAB$. If t is within 0 and 1, the point is on the line bounded by the two satellites, and if t is outside of that range, the point lies on the line but outside of the limits provided by the satellites.
